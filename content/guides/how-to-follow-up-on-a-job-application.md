Most students apply, wait, and hope. The application goes into a system, and the next thing they hear, if they hear anything, is an automated rejection six weeks later.

A short, well-timed follow-up email won't save a bad application. But it can get a good one read by a human being instead of sitting in a queue. Here's how to do it without being annoying.

## Why following up works

Recruiters at popular companies can receive hundreds of applications for a single internship. Many of those applications are never opened by a person; they're filtered, sorted, or simply not reached before the role fills.

A follow-up email does three things the application can't:

- **It puts your name in front of a person**, not just a database.
- **It shows genuine interest** in this specific role, which is rare when most candidates apply to dozens of jobs at once.
- **It gives you a second chance to make your case** in two or three sentences, in plain language.

The goal isn't to pressure anyone. It's to be the candidate a recruiter remembers when they finally sit down with the list.

## When to follow up

Timing matters more than wording.

1. **Apply first.** Always submit the official application before you email anyone. Recruiters can't do anything with you until you're in their system.
2. **Wait about five business days.** That's long enough that you aren't hovering, and short enough that the role probably isn't filled yet.
3. **Follow up once more, about a week later**, if you hear nothing. Then stop. Two emails is persistent; four is a pattern people remember for the wrong reason.

If the posting gave a specific timeline ("we'll be in touch by March 15"), wait until that date passes before following up.

## Who to send it to

The best person to email is someone who can actually move your application forward. In rough order of usefulness:

1. **The recruiter for the role.** Often named in the posting, on LinkedIn, or findable with a little digging. Here's [how to find the recruiter for any job](/guides/how-to-find-the-recruiter-for-a-job).
2. **The hiring manager or someone on the team.** A manager who wants the role filled can forward your name to the recruiter with one line.
3. **An alum or contact at the company.** They can't hire you, but they can often tell you who can, or pass your name along.

Avoid generic inboxes like jobs@ or careers@ when you can. They tend to be slow, and your email lands in the same pile as your application.

## Template 1: The standard follow-up

Use this about five business days after applying.

> **Subject:** Following up on my application for the Marketing Intern role
>
> Hi Sarah,
>
> I applied for the Marketing Intern position last week and wanted to follow up directly. I'm a junior at Colby studying economics, and I ran social media for a campus club that grew its following by about 40% this year.
>
> I'd love to bring that experience to your team. Would you be open to a quick conversation, or is there anything else I can send that would help?
>
> Thanks for your time,
> Jordan Lee

Notice what it does: names the role, gives one specific reason you're a fit, and asks a simple question. It's short enough to read on a phone.

## Template 2: The second follow-up

Send this about a week after the first one if you haven't heard back.

> **Subject:** Re: Following up on my application for the Marketing Intern role
>
> Hi Sarah,
>
> Just bumping this up in case it got buried. I'm still very interested in the Marketing Intern role, and I'm happy to work around your schedule if a short call would be useful.
>
> Best,
> Jordan

Reply in the same thread so the context stays attached. Keep it even shorter than the first.

## Template 3: Following up with someone on the team

When you're writing to a team member instead of a recruiter, the ask is different. You're not asking them to hire you; you're asking for a small, easy favor.

> **Subject:** Colby student interested in the analyst role on your team
>
> Hi Marcus,
>
> I recently applied for the Data Analyst internship on your team. I'm a sophomore studying statistics, and I built a small dashboard tracking campus dining wait times that a few hundred students now use.
>
> If you think I'd be a good fit, would you be willing to pass my name to whoever is hiring? Either way, I'd really appreciate any advice on the role.
>
> Thank you,
> Priya Shah

## What to include, and what to leave out

A strong follow-up email has:

- **A clear subject line** with the role name. "Following up" alone gets ignored.
- **One concrete detail about you** that connects to the job: a project, a number, a relevant class.
- **One easy ask**: a quick call, whether there's anything else to send, or a forward.
- **Fewer than 120 words.** If it scrolls on a phone, it's too long.

Leave out:

- Your entire resume pasted into the body.
- Apologies for "bothering" them. You aren't.
- Demands like "I'd like an update on my status."
- Generic flattery that could apply to any company.

## Keeping track of who you've followed up with

Following up only works if you actually remember to do it. Once you have more than ten applications going, it becomes easy to lose track of who you emailed and when the second follow-up is due.

At minimum, keep a simple list with the company, the role, the date you applied, the person you emailed, and the date your next follow-up is due. We walk through a full setup in [how to track your job applications](/guides/how-to-track-job-applications).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it okay to follow up on a job application?

Yes. A polite, short follow-up after about a week is normal and often welcomed. It shows interest and can get your application read by a person instead of a filter.

### How long should I wait to follow up after applying?

About five business days is a good default. If the posting gave a decision date, wait until after it passes.

### What if I can't find the recruiter's email?

Try someone on the team you'd join, a hiring manager, or an alum at the company. A LinkedIn message works too if you can't find an email address.

### How many times should I follow up?

Twice at most: once about five business days after applying, and once more a week later. After that, move on and put your energy into new applications.
