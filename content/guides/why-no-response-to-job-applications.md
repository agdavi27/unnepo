You find a role you're excited about. You spend an hour on the application. You hit Submit. And then nothing. No interview, no rejection, no sign that a human ever saw it.

If that sounds familiar, you're not alone, and it's usually not because you're unqualified. Most of the reasons applications vanish have nothing to do with you. The good news is that several of them are fixable.

## Why applications disappear

### Too many applicants, too few readers

Popular internships can attract hundreds or even thousands of applicants. A recruiter may be hiring for a dozen roles at once. Many applications are simply never opened, because the role fills from the first batch reviewed.

### Filters screen you out before a person does

Applicant tracking systems (ATS) let recruiters filter by keywords, graduation year, location, work authorization and answers to knockout questions. If your resume doesn't match what they search for, it may never come up. We explain how this works in [how applicant tracking systems read your resume](/guides/how-applicant-tracking-systems-read-resumes).

### The timing was off

Many companies review applications on a rolling basis. Applying three weeks after a posting goes up can mean applying after interviews have already started.

### The job wasn't really open

Some postings stay up after a role is filled, are kept open for future pipelines, or are posted because of internal rules even though there's already a preferred candidate. You can't control this one, which is why spreading your effort across many roles matters.

### Nobody ever writes back to say no

Plenty of companies never send rejections at all. Silence isn't always a no. It's often just no process.

## Seven changes that get more responses

### 1. Apply early

Set up job alerts and try to apply within the first few days of a posting going live. Early applications get reviewed while the recruiter is still actively reading. Late ones compete with a shortlist that already exists.

### 2. Mirror the job description

Use the same words the posting uses, where they're true for you. If the posting says "data visualization," don't only write "made charts." This helps with both keyword filters and the six-second skim a human gives your resume. Here's [a 10-minute way to tailor your resume](/guides/tailor-resume-to-job-description).

### 3. Lead with results, not duties

"Responsible for social media" says nothing. "Grew club Instagram from 300 to 1,100 followers in one semester" says a lot. Numbers make you memorable even when you don't have much experience.

### 4. Answer knockout questions carefully

Questions about work authorization, graduation date, location and availability are often used as automatic filters. Read them slowly. A wrong click on "Are you available to start in May?" can end your application instantly.

### 5. Find a person and follow up

This is the single biggest lever most students ignore. After applying, find the recruiter, the hiring manager, or an alum on the team and send a short, specific email. An application with a human advocate is far more likely to be read.

See [how to find the recruiter](/guides/how-to-find-the-recruiter-for-a-job) and our [follow-up templates](/guides/how-to-follow-up-on-a-job-application).

### 6. Apply to more of the right roles

Applying to five dream companies and waiting is a recipe for a stressful spring. You don't need hundreds of applications, but you do need enough volume that a few silent ones don't decide your summer. We cover realistic numbers in [how many internship applications you should send](/guides/how-many-internship-applications-should-i-send).

### 7. Track everything

When you're applying to many roles, it's easy to forget where you applied, who you contacted, and when to follow up. A simple tracker turns a chaotic process into a routine you can actually keep up with.

## How to tell if your resume is the problem

If you've sent 30 or more well-targeted applications and gotten zero interviews, it's worth looking hard at your resume:

- Ask your career center for a review.
- Ask a friend who's landed an internship in your field to compare their resume with yours.
- Check that it's a simple, one-column layout that copies cleanly into plain text.
- Make sure your strongest experience is at the top, not buried on page two.

If you're getting interviews but no offers, the issue is more likely interview prep than your application.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do I never hear back from job applications?

Usually because of volume, automated filters, or timing, not because you're unqualified. Applying earlier, tailoring your resume, and following up with a real person all improve your odds.

### How long should I wait to hear back after applying?

Many companies take two to four weeks, and some never respond. Follow up after about five business days, and keep applying elsewhere in the meantime.

### Does no response mean I was rejected?

Not necessarily. Many companies don't send rejections, and some postings go unreviewed for weeks. Treat silence as "not yet" and keep your pipeline moving.

### Should I reapply if I didn't hear back?

If the role is still posted after several weeks, you can reach out to a recruiter instead of reapplying. For a different role at the same company, applying again is fine.
