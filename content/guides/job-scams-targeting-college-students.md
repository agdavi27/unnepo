When you're applying to dozens of roles, a message offering a well-paid remote job with no interview can feel like a lucky break. Unfortunately, students are frequent targets of job scams, precisely because they're actively looking, often new to professional hiring, and eager for flexible work.

Here's how to recognize the most common scams and protect yourself.

## The most common scams

### Fake check scams

You're "hired" for a remote role such as an administrative assistant or personal assistant. The employer sends you a check to buy equipment or supplies, and asks you to send part of the money back or to a "vendor." The check later bounces, and you're responsible for the money you sent.

### Reshipping and package scams

You're asked to receive packages at home and reship them elsewhere. The goods are often bought with stolen credit cards, which can involve you in fraud.

### Fake recruiter messages

Scammers impersonate real companies on LinkedIn, Handshake, text or email, sometimes using real company names and logos. They may conduct "interviews" entirely by chat, then make an offer and ask for personal or banking information.

### Pay-to-work schemes

You're asked to pay for training, certification, a starter kit or a background check before you can begin.

### Data harvesting

A fake application asks for your Social Security number, bank details or a copy of your ID before any real interview, which can lead to identity theft.

## Red flags to watch for

- **An offer without a real interview**, or an interview conducted only over text or chat.
- **Pay that seems too high** for the work described.
- **Vague job descriptions** with little detail about the actual role.
- **Requests for money**, for any reason.
- **Checks or payments before you've started**, especially with instructions to send money elsewhere.
- **Pressure to act quickly**: "We need an answer today."
- **Personal email addresses** (like Gmail) for what claims to be a large company.
- **Requests for sensitive information early**, such as your Social Security number or bank account before an official offer.
- **Messages that start a conversation on one platform** and quickly move you to a messaging app.

## How to verify a job is real

1. **Find the role on the company's own careers page.** If it isn't listed there, be cautious.
2. **Check the recruiter's email domain.** It should match the company's real domain.
3. **Look up the recruiter on LinkedIn.** Are they connected to real employees? Does their profile have history?
4. **Contact the company directly** using contact information from its official website, not the message you received.
5. **Ask your career center.** They often know about scams circulating at your school.
6. **Search the company name plus "scam."** Others may have reported the same scheme.

## Applying safely

- **Apply through official company sites** whenever possible. See [where students should apply](/guides/handshake-vs-linkedin-vs-company-websites).
- **Never share your Social Security number or bank details** until you've verified the company and received an official written offer.
- **Never deposit a check and send money back** for a job.
- **Never pay to get a job.**
- **Keep a record** of who you've applied to, so an unexpected "offer" from a company you never contacted stands out. See [how to track your job applications](/guides/how-to-track-job-applications).

## If you think you've been scammed

- **Stop all communication** with the scammer.
- **Contact your bank** right away if you shared financial information or deposited a check.
- **Report it** to the platform where you found the posting, to your career center, and to the Federal Trade Commission at reportfraud.ftc.gov (in the U.S.).
- **Watch your accounts and credit** if you shared personal information, and consider a credit freeze.

Being targeted isn't a sign you did anything wrong. Scammers are good at what they do, and reporting helps protect other students.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can I tell if a job offer is a scam?

Watch for offers without real interviews, pay that seems too high, requests for money or sensitive information, pressure to act fast, and email addresses that don't match the company's domain.

### Do real employers ever send checks before you start?

Legitimate employers generally don't send checks for you to deposit and forward money from. That's a classic scam.

### Is it safe to give my Social Security number on a job application?

Only after you've verified that the company and role are real, typically once you've received an official offer and are completing hiring paperwork.

### Where do I report a job scam?

Report it to the platform where you found it, your career center, and in the U.S., the Federal Trade Commission at reportfraud.ftc.gov.
