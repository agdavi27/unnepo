Rejection is the most common outcome in any job search, even for students who end up with great offers. It still stings, especially after an interview you thought went well.

What you do in the hour, day and week after a rejection matters more than the rejection itself. Here's a simple plan.

## First, give yourself a moment

It's normal to feel disappointed, frustrated or embarrassed. Don't fire off a reply in the first few minutes, and don't rush to decide what it "means" about you. Close your laptop, take a walk, talk to a friend.

Then remember a few things that are almost always true:

- **Hiring is competitive.** Popular internships can have hundreds of qualified applicants for a few spots.
- **Many factors are out of your control**: internal candidates, budget changes, timing, or a team's very specific needs.
- **One rejection is one data point.** It isn't a verdict on your potential.

## Reply graciously (it's worth it)

Most students never respond to rejection emails. A short, gracious reply keeps the door open, and recruiters do remember candidates who handled disappointment well.

> **Subject:** Re: Your application for the Marketing Internship
>
> Hi Sarah,
>
> Thank you for letting me know, and for the time you and the team spent with me. I really enjoyed learning about the campus ambassador program, and I'd love to be considered for future opportunities.
>
> If you have a moment, I'd be grateful for any feedback on how I could strengthen my application.
>
> Best,
> Jordan Lee

Keep it short, positive and free of any pushback. Connect with the recruiter on LinkedIn afterward if you haven't already.

## Learn what you can

Whether or not you get feedback, take five minutes to reflect:

- **Where did it end?** No response after applying? Rejected after a phone screen? After a final round? Each stage points to something different.
- **What went well?** Keep doing it.
- **What would you change?** A weak answer, a skill gap, a resume that didn't match the role?

Patterns matter more than any single rejection:

- **Rejected without interviews over and over?** Your resume or targeting likely needs work. See [why you never hear back](/guides/why-no-response-to-job-applications).
- **Rejected after interviews?** Practice your answers. See [STAR method examples](/guides/star-method-examples-for-students).

## Keep your pipeline full

The best protection against the sting of one rejection is having several other things in motion. Students who send a steady stream of applications experience each "no" as a small setback rather than a crisis.

After a rejection:

1. **Apply to one or two new roles the same week.** Momentum is a great antidote to disappointment.
2. **Follow up on applications that are still open.**
3. **Reach out to one new person**, such as an alum or a recruiter.

If you need a sustainable routine, see [how to job search during a busy semester](/guides/job-search-during-the-semester).

## Should you reapply later?

Often, yes. Many people get hired by a company that turned them down before. If you were close, especially if you reached a final round, the company may already think well of you. See [can you reapply to a company that rejected you?](/guides/reapplying-to-a-company-after-rejection).

## When it keeps happening

A long string of rejections can wear anyone down. If you're struggling:

- **Talk to your career center** for an outside perspective.
- **Ask a friend in your field** to look over your materials.
- **Widen your search** to more kinds of companies and roles.
- **Take care of yourself.** Sleep, exercise and time with friends make you a stronger candidate too.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I reply to a rejection email?

Yes. A short, gracious reply thanking them and expressing interest in future opportunities keeps the relationship positive and can help if you apply again.

### Is it okay to ask for feedback after a rejection?

Yes, politely and briefly. Some companies don't share feedback, but asking never hurts if you're gracious.

### How do I stay motivated after being rejected?

Keep several applications in progress, apply to something new the same week, measure your effort instead of outcomes, and lean on friends and your career center.

### Does a rejection mean I can never work there?

No. Many people are hired by companies that rejected them earlier, often after gaining more experience.
