A cold email is a message to someone you don't know asking for an opportunity. For students, it's one of the few ways to get around the online application pile, and to find internships that are never posted at all.

Most cold emails fail for the same reasons: they're too long, too vague, or all about what the student wants. Here's how to write one that people actually answer.

## Why cold emailing works for students

Lots of smaller companies, startups, labs and nonprofits hire interns without ever posting a listing. They don't have recruiters or a careers page. When they need help, they hire someone who's already shown up in their inbox.

Even at bigger companies, a direct email to the right person can turn an application into a conversation. And because so few students do it well, a good cold email stands out immediately.

## Who to email

Pick someone who can actually say yes, or point you to someone who can:

- **At small companies and startups:** the founder, CEO, or head of the team you'd join.
- **At mid-size companies:** the manager of the relevant team.
- **At large companies:** a university recruiter, or an alum on the team.
- **At research labs:** the professor or principal investigator who runs the lab.

Avoid generic inboxes. If you need help finding a name or an address, see [how to find the recruiter for any job](/guides/how-to-find-the-recruiter-for-a-job).

## The structure that works

Every good cold email answers four questions in under 150 words:

1. **Who are you?** One line: year, school, and focus.
2. **Why them?** One specific sentence that shows you know what they do.
3. **Why you?** One concrete thing you've done that's relevant.
4. **What's the ask?** Small, clear, and easy to say yes to.

That's it. Everything else is extra weight.

## Template 1: Asking about an internship at a small company

> **Subject:** Colby sophomore interested in interning at Brightline
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> I'm a sophomore at Colby studying computer science. I've been using Brightline's scheduling app for my campus job, and I really liked how you simplified shift swaps in your last update.
>
> This past semester I built a small web app that helps my dorm split grocery orders, which taught me a lot about React and working with real users.
>
> Are you taking on any interns this summer? I'd love to help with front-end work, and I'm happy to start with a small project so you can see how I work.
>
> Thanks for reading,
> Alex Kim

## Template 2: Reaching out to a research lab

> **Subject:** Undergraduate interested in research in your lab
>
> Dear Professor Okafor,
>
> I'm a junior biology major at Colby. I read your recent paper on coral heat tolerance and was especially interested in how you measured recovery after bleaching events.
>
> I've completed coursework in marine ecology and statistics, and I spent last summer collecting water samples for a local watershed association.
>
> Would you have room for an undergraduate research assistant this summer? I'd be grateful for any opportunity to contribute, including data entry or field support.
>
> Sincerely,
> Maya Torres

## Template 3: Writing to an alum at a large company

> **Subject:** Fellow Colby student, quick question about your team
>
> Hi Chris,
>
> I'm a junior at Colby, and I saw that you're on the strategy team at Northwind. I'm applying for their summer analyst program and would love to hear what the work is actually like.
>
> Would you have 15 minutes for a call in the next couple of weeks? I know you're busy, so I'm happy to work around your schedule.
>
> Thank you,
> Sam Patel

## Subject lines that get opened

Your subject line decides whether the email gets read. Keep it short and specific:

- "Colby junior interested in your summer internship"
- "Question about the analyst role on your team"
- "Fellow Colby student, quick question"
- "Undergraduate interested in research in your lab"

Avoid vague subjects like "Hello," "Opportunity," or "Internship inquiry," which look like spam.

## Mistakes that get cold emails ignored

- **Writing a wall of text.** If it doesn't fit on a phone screen, cut it.
- **Making it all about you.** "I'm looking for experience to build my resume" gives them no reason to help.
- **Asking for too much.** "Can you get me a job?" is hard to answer. "Are you taking interns?" is easy.
- **Using a template without changing it.** People can tell. One specific sentence about them changes everything.
- **Attaching your resume without mentioning it.** If you attach it, say so in one line.

## How many cold emails to send

Cold emailing is a numbers game with a quality floor. Sending five carefully written emails a week is far more effective than blasting fifty generic ones in a day. Many won't get a reply, and that's normal.

Follow up once after about five business days if you hear nothing. A short "just bumping this up" in the same thread is enough. Our [follow-up guide](/guides/how-to-follow-up-on-a-job-application) covers timing in detail.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it okay to cold email for an internship?

Yes. Many companies, especially small ones, hire interns entirely through people who reached out first. A short, respectful email is professional, not pushy.

### Should I attach my resume to a cold email?

Attaching a PDF resume is usually fine, as long as you mention it in one line. For research labs and professors, it's expected.

### What should the subject line of a cold email say?

Keep it short and specific: your year and school plus what you're asking about, such as "Colby junior interested in your summer internship."

### What if nobody replies to my cold emails?

That's normal. Follow up once, then move on to new contacts. Look at your emails again: shorter, more specific messages with an easier ask tend to get more replies.
