"Tell me about a time you worked on a team." "Tell me about a time you faced a challenge." Behavioral questions are a staple of internship interviews, and they're where many students freeze up or ramble.

The STAR method gives you a simple structure so your answers are clear, specific and about the right length.

## What STAR stands for

- **Situation:** Set the scene in one or two sentences. Where were you, and what was going on?
- **Task:** What were you responsible for? What was the goal or problem?
- **Action:** What did *you* specifically do? This is the most important part.
- **Result:** What happened? Use numbers if you can, and mention what you learned.

Aim for about 60 to 90 seconds per answer, and spend the most time on Action.

## Where students find their stories

You don't need internship experience to answer behavioral questions well. Strong stories come from:

- Group projects and research papers
- Campus jobs and part-time work
- Clubs, student government and Greek life
- Sports teams
- Volunteering
- Personal projects

Before an interview, prepare five or six stories you can adapt to different questions.

## Five example answers

### "Tell me about a time you worked on a team."

> **Situation:** In my sophomore marketing class, I was in a group of four building a campaign for a local bakery.
>
> **Task:** Two weeks before the deadline, we realized we had four separate ideas and no shared plan.
>
> **Action:** I suggested we each pitch our idea in five minutes, then vote on criteria we agreed on first: budget, target audience and how measurable it was. I set up a shared doc with deadlines for each section and checked in with everyone twice a week.
>
> **Result:** We finished three days early, got an A, and the bakery owner actually used our Instagram plan. I learned that agreeing on how to decide matters as much as the decision itself.

### "Tell me about a time you faced a challenge."

> **Situation:** I was working at the campus library desk when our checkout system went down during finals week.
>
> **Task:** Students still needed reserved textbooks, and we had a line out the door.
>
> **Action:** I set up a paper sign-out sheet with student IDs and due dates, asked a coworker to manage the line while I handled checkouts, and posted a sign explaining the delay.
>
> **Result:** We kept checkouts moving for two hours with no lost books, and my supervisor adopted the paper backup as an official procedure.

### "Tell me about a time you showed leadership."

> **Situation:** Our environmental club had about eight active members and dwindling attendance.
>
> **Task:** As the newly elected vice president, I wanted to rebuild interest.
>
> **Action:** I surveyed students who had attended once but never returned. Most said meetings felt like lectures. So I replaced half our meetings with hands-on events like trail cleanups and a clothing swap.
>
> **Result:** Regular attendance grew to about 25 people by spring, and the clothing swap became an annual event.

### "Tell me about a time you made a mistake."

> **Situation:** As treasurer of my club, I submitted a funding request to student government.
>
> **Task:** I needed approval for our spring speaker event.
>
> **Action:** I missed the submission deadline by a day because I had the wrong date in my calendar. I emailed the finance committee right away, owned the mistake, and asked if there was any late process. There was, so I submitted the next morning with a clear budget breakdown.
>
> **Result:** The event was funded. Since then, I set calendar reminders a week before every deadline and double-check dates against the original source.

### "Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly."

> **Situation:** For a research assistant position, my professor asked me to analyze survey data in R. I had only used Excel.
>
> **Task:** I had two weeks to produce summary statistics and charts.
>
> **Action:** I worked through an online R course in the evenings, practiced on a sample dataset, and asked a graduate student to review my code once.
>
> **Result:** I delivered the analysis on time, and my professor used two of my charts in a conference presentation.

## Tips for delivering STAR answers

- **Say "I," not "we,"** when describing your actions. Interviewers want to know what you did.
- **Don't memorize scripts.** Remember the key beats of each story instead.
- **Be honest about results.** Not every story needs a big win, but it should have a lesson.
- **Practice out loud** with a friend or your career center. See [how to get the most out of your career center](/guides/how-to-use-your-college-career-center).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the STAR method?

A structure for answering behavioral interview questions: Situation, Task, Action and Result. It keeps answers focused and specific.

### How long should a STAR answer be?

About 60 to 90 seconds, with most of the time spent on the Action part.

### Can I use school projects for STAR answers?

Yes. Class projects, campus jobs, clubs, sports and volunteering are all great sources, especially for internship interviews.

### How many STAR stories should I prepare?

Prepare five or six strong stories. Most can be adapted to answer several different questions.
