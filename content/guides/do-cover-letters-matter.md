Ask around and you'll hear both answers with total confidence: "Nobody reads cover letters" and "A great cover letter got me the job." Both can be true. Whether a cover letter matters depends a lot on where you're applying and how you use it.

## The honest answer: sometimes, and you can't always tell when

Many recruiters skim or skip cover letters, especially for high-volume programs. But some hiring managers read every one, particularly at smaller companies, nonprofits, research labs and anywhere writing matters to the job. And you rarely know in advance which kind of reader you'll get.

So the real question isn't "do cover letters matter?" It's "is a cover letter worth the time for this specific application?"

## When a cover letter clearly helps

- **When it's required.** If the application says "required," skipping it can disqualify you or signal that you didn't read the instructions.
- **At small companies and startups.** A founder or manager is often the one reading applications, and they care about fit and motivation.
- **For writing-heavy roles.** Communications, marketing, journalism, policy, publishing and research all treat your letter as a writing sample.
- **When your resume needs context.** Switching fields, applying from a small school, an unusual background, or a gap that needs explaining.
- **When you have a real connection.** If an alum referred you or you met the recruiter at an event, the cover letter is the place to say so.

## When you can usually skip it

- **When it's optional on a high-volume application**, such as large internship programs with thousands of applicants and structured screening questions.
- **When there's no place to submit one.** Don't force it into a resume upload field.
- **When a generic letter is the only option you have time for.** A bland letter adds nothing; your time may be better spent finding a recruiter to email.

## The better use of your time: a follow-up email

For many applications, the cover letter is a letter to no one in particular, uploaded into a system. A short email to the actual recruiter or hiring manager after you apply often does more, because it reaches a real person in their inbox.

Think of it as a mini cover letter: two or three sentences about why you fit, sent directly to someone who can act on it. Our [follow-up guide](/guides/how-to-follow-up-on-a-job-application) has templates.

## How to write a good cover letter quickly

If you decide a cover letter is worth it, you don't need to start from zero every time:

1. **Write one strong base letter** with your best story and a clear structure.
2. **Rewrite the first paragraph for each company**, with one specific reason you want to work there.
3. **Swap your example** for the one most relevant to the role.
4. **Keep it under 350 words.**

## What makes a cover letter worth reading

Readers notice letters that:

- **Open with something specific**, not "I am writing to express my interest."
- **Tell one concrete story** rather than listing adjectives.
- **Show they understand the role** and the company.
- **Are short.** Respecting the reader's time is part of the impression.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do recruiters actually read cover letters?

Some do and some don't. Readers at small companies, research labs and writing-heavy roles are more likely to. For large high-volume programs, many recruiters focus on the resume and screening questions.

### Should I submit a cover letter if it's optional?

If you can write a specific, strong letter, yes. It can only help with readers who value it. If you'd only be sending a generic one, a follow-up email to a real person may be a better use of your time.

### Is a bad cover letter worse than none?

Often, yes. A letter with typos, the wrong company name, or generic filler can hurt an application that would have been fine without it.

### How long should a cover letter be?

About 250 to 350 words. Under one page, always.
