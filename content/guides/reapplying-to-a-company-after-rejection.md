Getting rejected from a company you really wanted is rough. But a rejection is rarely permanent. Plenty of people get hired by companies that turned them down the first time, sometimes just months later.

Here's how to know when to try again, and how to make your second application stronger than your first.

## Yes, you can usually reapply

Most companies are happy to see strong candidates apply again. Recruiters know that a student who wasn't quite ready in sophomore year might be a great fit as a junior. Hiring decisions also depend on timing, team needs and the competition in a particular cycle, none of which reflect on you permanently.

Some exceptions to keep in mind:

- **Waiting periods.** Some companies ask candidates to wait a set period, often six months to a year, before reapplying for the same role. Check the careers page or ask the recruiter.
- **Same posting, same cycle.** Reapplying to the exact posting you were just rejected from rarely changes anything.

## Different role versus the same role

- **A different role** at the same company is usually fine right away, as long as it's a genuine fit.
- **The same role** in a later cycle (next year's internship, for instance) is a good opportunity, especially if you've grown in the meantime.
- **Applying to everything** at one company at once can look unfocused. Pick the roles you actually fit.

## Use your first rejection

### Ask for feedback

If you interviewed, it's reasonable to send a short, gracious note asking for feedback:

> Hi Sarah,
>
> Thank you for letting me know, and for the chance to interview for the Marketing Internship. I really enjoyed meeting the team. If you have a moment, I'd be grateful for any feedback on how I could strengthen my candidacy for future roles. I hope to apply again.
>
> Best,
> Jordan Lee

Many companies won't share details, but some recruiters will, and even a single sentence can be valuable.

### Stay in touch

That same message keeps the door open. Connect with the recruiter on LinkedIn. A recruiter who remembers you positively can flag your next application.

## Make your second application stronger

When you reapply, show what's changed:

- **New experience:** a relevant internship, job, research or project.
- **New skills:** a course, certification, or tool you've learned.
- **Better targeting:** a resume tailored more closely to the role. See [how to tailor your resume in 10 minutes](/guides/tailor-resume-to-job-description).
- **Better preparation:** if you struggled in interviews, practice before the next cycle. See [STAR method examples](/guides/star-method-examples-for-students).
- **A connection:** an alum or team member who can vouch for you. See [how to ask for a referral](/guides/how-to-ask-for-a-referral).

In your cover letter or outreach email, it's fine to mention briefly that you applied before and what you've done since:

> I applied for your internship last year, and since then I've completed a data analysis project for a local nonprofit and taken coursework in SQL. I'm even more excited about the role now.

## Don't put all your hopes on one company

Reapplying to a dream company makes sense, but keep it in perspective. Your long-term success doesn't depend on any single employer. Keep a broad pipeline of applications while you wait for the next cycle. For dealing with the emotional side, see [what to do after a rejection email](/guides/what-to-do-after-a-job-rejection).

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I wait to reapply to a company?

For the same role, wait until the next hiring cycle or until any stated waiting period passes, often six months to a year. For a different role that fits you, you can usually apply right away.

### Will a company remember that I was rejected before?

They may have your previous application on file. That's usually fine, and it's an opportunity to show how you've grown.

### Should I mention my previous application?

Briefly, yes, if it helps tell a story of growth. Focus on what you've done since, not on the rejection.

### Should I ask for feedback after a rejection?

It's reasonable to ask politely, especially after an interview. Not every company will share feedback, but it keeps the relationship positive.
