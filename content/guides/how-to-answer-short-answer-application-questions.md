Many applications now include written questions: "Why are you interested in this role?" "Describe a time you solved a difficult problem." "What's something you're proud of?" They're often limited to 100 to 300 words, and they can matter as much as your resume.

They're also where many students rush, paste something generic, and lose ground. Here's how to write answers that stand out without spending hours on each one.

## Why short answers matter

Short answers give recruiters something your resume can't: your voice, your reasoning, and evidence that you actually want this job. When two candidates have similar resumes, a specific, thoughtful answer can be the tiebreaker.

They're also used for screening. A vague or off-topic answer can end an application early.

## The simple structure

Most short-answer questions can be answered with three moves:

1. **Answer the question directly** in your first sentence.
2. **Back it up with a specific example** from your experience.
3. **Connect it to the role** or company.

This keeps your answers focused even under tight word limits.

## Common questions and how to approach them

### "Why are you interested in this role/company?"

Name something specific about the company, connect it to your experience or interests, and say what you'd contribute. See our full guide on [how to answer "Why do you want to work here?"](/guides/why-do-you-want-to-work-here-answer).

### "Describe a challenge you faced and how you handled it."

Use a compressed STAR structure: one sentence of situation, one of task, two or three on what you did, and one on the result. See [STAR method examples](/guides/star-method-examples-for-students).

### "What's an accomplishment you're proud of?"

Choose something with a clear result, and explain why it mattered to you. The "why" is what makes the answer personal.

### "Tell us about yourself."

Give a brief, focused snapshot: your studies, one or two experiences that relate to the role, and what you're looking for. It's not your life story.

### "Is there anything else you'd like us to know?"

Optional, but useful. Use it to add context, such as a strong project not on your resume, a connection to the company, or a brief explanation of something unusual in your background. If you have nothing meaningful to add, it's fine to skip.

## An example

Question: "Describe a project you're proud of." (150 words)

> The project I'm proudest of is a dining hall wait-time tracker I built last spring. Students at my school constantly complained about lunch lines, but nobody knew when the busy times actually were.
>
> I collected two weeks of swipe data from dining services, cleaned it in Google Sheets, and built a simple model predicting peak times by day and hour. I shared a one-page summary with dining staff and posted a chart for students.
>
> Dining services adjusted staffing during the two busiest windows, and lines at peak lunch got noticeably shorter. It's the first time I saw data lead directly to a change people could feel. That's exactly why your analyst role, turning sales data into decisions for store managers, appeals to me.

## Build an answer bank

You'll see the same questions over and over. Save time by keeping a document with:

- Your best answers to the most common questions.
- Five or six STAR stories you can adapt.
- A few sentences about your interests and goals.
- Notes on specific companies you've researched.

For each new application, start from your bank and customize the parts that should be specific to the company. This is one of the biggest time-savers in a high-volume search. See [how to job search during a busy semester](/guides/job-search-during-the-semester).

## Tips for every answer

- **Respect the word limit.** Going over can get cut off; going far under can look low-effort.
- **Be specific.** Names, numbers and real details beat adjectives.
- **Write like a person.** Clear, natural sentences, no buzzwords. If you use AI to help, rewrite it in your own voice. See [how to use AI to apply for jobs](/guides/using-ai-to-apply-for-jobs).
- **Proofread.** Write your answers in a document first, then paste them into the form.
- **Answer the question they asked**, not the one you prepared for.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should short answers on job applications be?

Use most of the word limit you're given. If there's no limit, 100 to 200 words is usually plenty.

### Can I reuse answers across applications?

Yes, as a starting point. Customize the parts that mention the company or role, and make sure the answer fits the exact question.

### What if I don't have a strong example?

Look at class projects, campus jobs, clubs, sports and personal projects. Small situations described with specific detail often work better than big, vague ones.

### Should I answer optional questions?

Answer them if you have something meaningful to add. A thoughtful optional answer can help; a filler answer won't.
